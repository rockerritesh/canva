1Meaning of engineering economics[3]
“Engineering economics is the application of minimum resources to generate maximum benefits”. Justify the statement with a suitable example.
Solution
Resources such as money, material, labour, machine time and energy are always limited. But our needs are many. An engineering problem can usually be solved in several ways that all work technically. Engineering economics gives us tools to find the way that gives the most benefit for the least resources. So the statement is true.

Definition

Engineering economics is the study of how to compare engineering alternatives in money terms (using interest, or the time value of money) and choose the one that is most economical.

Why the statement is correct

  1. Scarcity: every project has a fixed budget. Wasting money on one design means less money for other works.
  2. Many alternatives: a bridge can be RCC or steel; water can come from a pump or a gravity pipe. All work, but their costs differ.
  3. Fair comparison: costs and benefits come at different times. Using interest, we bring them to one point in time (PW, AW or FW) so they can be compared.
  4. Selection rule: choose the alternative with the highest net benefit, or the lowest cost for the same benefit (methods: PW, AW, IRR, B/C ratio, payback).
  1. Define the problem
  2. List the alternatives
  3. Estimate cash flows
  4. Evaluate at MARR (PW, AW, IRR, B/C)
  5. Choose the best and follow up

Example

A rural municipality must supply drinking water to a village for 10 years. Two options give the same amount of water. MARR = 10%, (P/A, 10%, 10) = 6.1446.

OptionFirst costRunning cost per yearPW of total cost
Deep tube well with electric pumpRs 20 lakhRs 2 lakh20 + 2 × 6.1446 = Rs 32.29 lakh
Gravity pipeline from a springRs 25 lakhRs 0.3 lakh25 + 0.3 × 6.1446 = Rs 26.84 lakh

The tube well looks cheaper at the start. But over its life the gravity scheme saves about Rs 5.45 lakh (in present worth). The benefit is the same, and the resources used are less. Without an economic study, the wrong option could be chosen.

Engineering economics compares alternatives with the time value of money and picks the one that gives the maximum benefit for the minimum resources. In the example, the gravity pipeline gives the same water at a PW cost of Rs 26.84 lakh instead of Rs 32.29 lakh. So the statement is justified.
2(a)Nominal vs effective rate · gradient fund[2+4]
Elaborate nominal and effective interest rate with a suitable example. A new machine is expected to cost Rs. 6,000 and have a life of 5 years. Maintenance costs will be Rs. 1,500 the first year, Rs. 1,700 the second year, Rs. 1,900 the third year, Rs. 2,100 the fourth year, and Rs. 2,300 the fifth year. How much should be deposited in a fund that earns 9% per year, compounded monthly, in order to pay for this machine?
Solution

Part 1: Nominal and effective interest rate [2]

Banks often say “12% per year, compounded monthly”. The 12% is only the name of the rate (nominal). Because interest is added every month and then itself earns interest, you really get a little more than 12% in a year. That real yearly rate is the effective rate.
Nominal rate (r)

The yearly rate that is stated or quoted. It does not count the effect of compounding inside the year. Example: “12% per year compounded monthly” has r = 12%.

Rate per period = r/m (here 12%/12 = 1% per month).

Effective rate (i)

The actual rate earned or paid in one year after interest is compounded m times in the year.

i = (1 + r/m)m − 1. For continuous compounding, i = er − 1.

Example: Rs 10,000 is deposited at 12% per year compounded monthly. After one year: 10,000 × (1.01)12 = Rs 11,268.25. The interest is Rs 1,268.25, so the effective rate is 12.68%, not 12%.

  • If m = 1 (yearly compounding), effective rate = nominal rate.
  • If m > 1, effective rate is greater than nominal rate.
  • More compounding gives a higher effective rate, but it can never go above er − 1 (continuous).

Part 2: Fund needed for the machine [4]

The fund must pay the price of the machine now and all five maintenance bills later. So the deposit needed today = present worth (PW) of all these costs. Maintenance grows by the same Rs 200 each year, so it is a uniform series plus an arithmetic gradient.
First cost = Rs 6,000 (now, t = 0) · Maintenance at end of years 1–5: 1,500, 1,700, 1,900, 2,100, 2,300 → A = Rs 1,500, G = Rs 200 · N = 5 years · r = 9% per year compounded monthly (m = 12).

Step 1: Effective annual interest rate

Costs come once a year, but interest is added 12 times a year. So we change 9% (monthly) into a yearly effective rate.

i = (1 + r/m)m − 1
i = (1 + 0.09/12)12 − 1 = (1.0075)12 − 1
i = 0.093807 = 9.3807% per year

2(b)Geometric gradient · equivalent annual deposit[4]
While you are planning to deposit Rs. 5,000 in 3 months interval for 4 years in increasing trend at a 2.5% growth rate per deposit, a bank enticing you with an interest rate of 10% per annum compounded semi-annually. What will be the equivalent equal annual deposit of that money?
Solution
The deposits are made every quarter and each one is 2.5% bigger than the one before. This is a geometric gradient (a series that grows by a fixed percentage). We first find its present worth using a quarterly rate. Then we spread that present worth into 4 equal yearly deposits using the yearly effective rate.
First deposit A1 = Rs 5,000 (end of first quarter) · growth g = 2.5% per deposit · N = 4 years × 4 = 16 deposits · r = 10% per year compounded semi-annually (5% per half-year).

Step 1: Interest rate per quarter

Interest is added every 6 months at 5%. A quarter is half of 6 months, so we take the square root of 1.05.

iq = (1.05)1/2 − 1 = 0.024695 = 2.4695% per quarter

Step 2: Present worth of the growing deposits

Here g (2.5%) is not equal to iq (2.4695%), so we use the normal geometric gradient formula.

P = A1 × [1 − ((1+g)/(1+i))N] / (i − g)
(1+g)/(1+i) = 1.025 / 1.024695 = 1.0002976
(1.0002976)16 = 1.0047718
P = 5,000 × (1 − 1.0047718) / (0.024695 − 0.025)
P = 5,000 × (−0.0047718) / (−0.00030492)
P = 5,000 × 15.6493 = Rs 78,246.49

Step 3: Effective yearly rate

ia = (1.05)2 − 1 = 0.1025 = 10.25% per year

Step 4: Equal annual deposit for 4 years

A = P × (A/P, 10.25%, 4)    (A/P, i, N) = i(1+i)N / [(1+i)N − 1]
(1.1025)4 = 1.477455
(A/P, 10.25%, 4) = 0.1025 × 1.477455 / 0.477455 = 0.31718
A = 78,246.49 × 0.31718 = Rs 24,818.20

Check through future worth

F at end of year 4 = 78,246.49 × (1.05)8 = Rs 1,15,605.70
(F/A, 10.25%, 4) = 0.477455 / 0.1025 = 4.6581
A = 1,15,605.70 / 4.6581 = Rs 24,818.20 ✓
The two rates (g = 2.5% and i = 2.4695%) are very close, so it is easy to think they are equal. If they were exactly equal, we would use P = N A1 / (1 + i) instead.
The 16 growing quarterly deposits are equal to Rs 24,818.20 per year deposited at the end of each year for 4 years (PW = Rs 78,246.49).

5(a)Replacement: cash flow vs opportunity cost[4]
Explain cash flow and opportunity cost approach of replacement analysis with suitable example.
Solution
In replacement analysis we compare the old asset (defender) with a new asset (challenger). The only difficult item is the money we can get by selling the old asset today. The two approaches just put this money in different places. Both give the same decision when done correctly.
Cash flow approach

The sale value of the defender is treated as a cash inflow that reduces the cost of the challenger.

  • Net investment for replacing = price of challenger − market value of defender.
  • We look at the difference in cash flows (savings) from replacing.
  • Defender has no first cost in this view.
  • Works well when both have the same remaining life.
Opportunity cost approach (outsider view)

The market value of the defender is treated as its investment cost, because by keeping it we give up (lose the chance to get) that money.

  • Defender first cost = its present market value.
  • Challenger first cost = its full price.
  • Find the AEC (equivalent annual cost) of each and choose the lower.
  • Preferred method, also for unequal lives.

Example (i = 10%, both kept 4 years)

Old machine: market value now Rs 30,000, O&M Rs 20,000 per year, salvage after 4 years Rs 5,000. New machine: price Rs 80,000, O&M Rs 6,000 per year, salvage after 4 years Rs 20,000. (A/P, 10%, 4) = 0.31547, (P/A, 10%, 4) = 3.1699, (P/F, 10%, 4) = 0.6830.

Opportunity cost approach: CR = (P − S)(A/P, i, N) + S·i

AEC(old) = (30,000 − 5,000)(0.31547) + 5,000(0.10) + 20,000
        = 7,886.77 + 500 + 20,000 = Rs 28,386.77
AEC(new) = (80,000 − 20,000)(0.31547) + 20,000(0.10) + 6,000
        = 18,928.25 + 2,000 + 6,000 = Rs 26,928.25
New machine is cheaper by Rs 1,458.52 per year → replace

Cash flow approach: net investment = 80,000 − 30,000 = 50,000; yearly saving = 20,000 − 6,000 = 14,000; extra salvage = 20,000 − 5,000 = 15,000.

PW = −50,000 + 14,000(3.1699) + 15,000(0.6830)
PW = −50,000 + 44,378.12 + 10,245.20 = +Rs 4,623.32 → replace
AW = 4,623.32 × 0.31547 = Rs 1,458.52 (same as above)
Common mistake: do not subtract the old machine’s sale value from the new price and also give the old machine a first cost. That counts the same money twice.
The cash flow approach uses the defender’s sale value to reduce the challenger’s investment and studies the net savings. The opportunity cost approach charges the defender its market value as an investment and compares AECs. In the example both give the same result: replace, with a saving of Rs 1,458.52 per year.

6(a)Scenario analysis · risk-adjusted MARR[4]
Explain with suitable example, how scenario analysis and risk-adjusted MARR can be used for quantitative risk analysis.
Solution
Future cash flows are only estimates, so every project has risk (a chance that the result is worse than expected). Quantitative risk analysis puts a number on this risk. Two simple tools are: scenario analysis (work out the result for a bad, normal and good future) and risk-adjusted MARR (ask a risky project to earn a higher rate).

1. Scenario analysis

We make three sets of estimates: pessimistic (worst case), most likely (normal), and optimistic (best case). We find the PW (or AW, IRR) for each. The spread of results shows how risky the project is.

Example: Investment Rs 10,00,000, life 5 years, MARR 12%, (P/A, 12%, 5) = 3.6048.

ScenarioNet revenue per yearPW = −10,00,000 + A × 3.6048
Pessimistic2,20,000−2,06,949
Most likely3,20,000+1,53,528
Optimistic4,00,000+4,41,910

The project is good in the normal and best cases, but loses about Rs 2.07 lakh in the worst case. If the firm cannot bear such a loss, it may reject the project or take steps to reduce risk. (If chances of 25%, 50%, 25% are given, the expected PW = Rs 1,35,505.)

2. Risk-adjusted MARR

A risky project must earn more to be accepted. So we add a risk premium to the normal MARR:

Risk-adjusted MARR = normal (risk-free) MARR + risk premium

Example: Normal MARR = 10%. A new, risky product gets a premium of 6%, so its MARR = 16%. Investment Rs 10,00,000, net revenue Rs 2,90,000 per year for 5 years.

At 10%: PW = −10,00,000 + 2,90,000 × 3.7908 = +99,328 (accept)
At 16%: PW = −10,00,000 + 2,90,000 × 3.2743 = −50,455 (reject)

With the risk-adjusted rate, the risky project fails. So the method protects the firm from risky projects that only look good at the normal rate.

Scenario analysis

Shows the full range of outcomes. Easy to explain. But it does not say how likely each case is (unless chances are added).

Risk-adjusted MARR

Very simple, one number. But the premium is chosen by judgment, and it treats far-future cash flows as more and more risky.

Scenario analysis measures risk by finding PW for worst, likely and best cases (here −2.07 lakh to +4.42 lakh). Risk-adjusted MARR handles risk by raising the required rate (here 10% → 16% turns PW from +99,328 to −50,455). Both help the firm decide whether the extra risk is worth taking.

7(a)MACRS and its assumptions[4]
What is MACRS? List out the assumptions made for calculating MACRS percentage.
Solution
Depreciation is the fall in value of an asset over time, which a firm can deduct as a cost for tax purposes. MACRS is a fixed set of rules (and ready-made yearly percentages) that tells you how much depreciation you may claim each year.

What is MACRS?

MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) is the depreciation system of the USA (since 1986) used for tax purposes. Every asset is put into a property class (3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20 years …) called the recovery period. Depreciation for year k is:

dk = (MACRS rate for year k) × cost basis B

It is “accelerated” because it gives larger depreciation in the early years. This lowers taxes early and improves the firm’s cash flow.

Assumptions used to build the MACRS percentages

  1. Salvage value is zero. The whole cost basis is depreciated, whatever the real salvage value.
  2. Half-year convention. The asset is assumed to be bought in the middle of the first year and sold in the middle of the last year. So only half a year’s depreciation is taken in year 1 and in the year after the recovery period ends. (A 5-year asset is depreciated over 6 tax years.)
  3. Declining balance method first: 200% declining balance (DDB) for 3, 5, 7 and 10-year classes; 150% declining balance for 15 and 20-year classes.
  4. Switch to straight line in the year when straight-line depreciation on the remaining book value becomes larger than declining balance.
  5. Recovery period comes from the property class (GDS table), not from the actual useful life of the asset.
  6. The rates are applied to the original (unadjusted) cost basis, which includes purchase price plus installation and transport.

8Inflation terms · average inflation[3+2]

Solution

Part 1: Definitions [3]

When prices go up, the same rupee buys less. Interest rates can be stated with this price rise included or removed. The three terms below describe these rates.
  1. Inflation rate (f): the yearly rate at which the general price level of goods and services goes up. Money loses buying power at this rate. Example: a bag of cement costs Rs 1,000 this year and Rs 1,060 next year, so f = 6%.
  2. Market interest rate (im), also called combined or nominal rate: the rate actually quoted by banks and lenders. It includes both the real earning power of money and the inflation. It is used with actual (then-current) rupees.
  3. Inflation-free interest rate (i′), also called real rate: the rate that shows only the real earning power of money, with the effect of inflation removed. It is used with constant (base-year) rupees.
1 + im = (1 + i′)(1 + f)  ⇒  im = i′ + f + i′·f    i′ = (im − f) / (1 + f)

Example: If i′ = 5% and f = 6%, then im = 0.05 + 0.06 + 0.05 × 0.06 = 0.113 = 11.3%.

Price rises build on each other (like compound interest), so the average rate is the geometric mean, not the simple average.

1(a)Cash flow diagram · why engineers need economics[4]
Define cash flow diagram. Why study of economics is essential for engineers?
Solution
A cash flow diagram is a picture of money over time. Engineers design things that cost money now and give benefits later, so they must know if the design is worth the money. That is why they study economics.

Part 1: Cash flow diagram

A cash flow diagram (CFD) is a graph that shows all the money coming in and going out of a project, and when it happens. It has:

  • A horizontal time line divided into equal periods (years, months). Point 0 is "now". Point 1 is the end of period 1, and so on.
  • Upward arrows for money received (cash inflow: income, savings, salvage value).
  • Downward arrows for money paid (cash outflow: investment, operating cost).
  • The length of an arrow roughly shows the size of the amount. Each arrow is labelled with the amount.
  • The interest rate (i) is written on the diagram.

We normally use the end-of-period convention: money that flows during a year is shown at the end of that year. The diagram depends on the viewpoint: a loan is an upward arrow for the borrower and a downward arrow for the bank.

Part 2: Why economics is essential for engineers

Engineering economics is the study of how to choose the best engineering option using money values and the time value of money. It is needed because:

  1. Limited resources. Money, material and labour are limited. The engineer must use them in the cheapest way that still does the job.
  2. Many technical options. A road can be built with asphalt or concrete; a pump can be bought or leased. Economics tells which option gives the most value.
  3. Time value of money. Rs 1 today is worth more than Rs 1 next year. Costs and benefits come at different times, so they must be compared with interest (PW, AW, IRR, B/C ratio).
  4. Project justification. Clients, banks and the government ask "Is it worth it?" before funding. The engineer must show feasibility in money terms.
  5. Replacement and planning. Knowing when to replace old machines, how to depreciate assets, and how inflation and taxes affect a project.
  6. Handling risk. Sensitivity, break-even and risk analysis help make safe decisions when the future is uncertain.

A design that works technically but loses money is a failed design. Good engineering = technically sound + economically feasible.

A CFD shows the amount and timing of all cash inflows (up arrows) and outflows (down arrows) on a time line. Engineers study economics so that their technically good designs are also the cheapest and most profitable choice, using the time value of money.

Part 1: Impact of inflation on economic evaluation

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. It reduces the purchasing power of money. In project evaluation it has these effects:

  1. Two kinds of rupees. Actual (then-current) rupees are the amounts really paid in the future. Constant (real) rupees are amounts in today’s buying power. Actual = Constant × (1 + f)n.
  2. Two kinds of interest rate. Market rate im (includes inflation) and real rate i′ (no inflation). They are linked by im = i′ + f + i′·f.

  5. Fixed amounts lose value. Loan payments, fixed contract prices and depreciation are fixed in actual rupees. Their real value falls with inflation. Inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.

  1. Goal is wealth. The firm wants the most rupees of wealth, not the highest %. NPV measures wealth directly.
  2. Realistic reinvestment. NPV assumes money is reinvested at MARR, which the firm can really earn. IRR assumes reinvestment at 30% or 22%, which is usually not possible.

7(a)Replacement analysis · infinite planning horizon[4]
Explain the replacement analysis under infinite planning horizon.
Solution
An infinite planning horizon means the service is needed forever (or for a very long time). So we will keep replacing machines again and again. The question is only: when should the old machine be replaced by the new one?

In replacement analysis, the defender is the asset we have now and the challenger is the possible new asset. Under an infinite horizon we assume:

  • The service is needed for an indefinite period.
  • The challenger, once bought, will be replaced by identical challengers forever, each kept for its economic service life (ESL).
  • Cash flows of future challengers stay the same (no technology change or inflation).

Because each challenger cycle repeats, its yearly cost forever equals its minimum EUAC, called AE*C. So the decision uses annual equivalent (AE) values. Steps:

  1. Find the ESL of the challenger and its minimum annual cost AE*C.
  2. Find the remaining ESL of the defender and its minimum annual cost AE*D (use the defender’s current market value as its investment: opportunity cost approach).
  3. If AE*D > AE*C: replace the defender now.
  4. If AE*D ≤ AE*C: keep the defender at least for now. Then each year compute the marginal cost of keeping it one more year: (MVstart − MVend) + i × MVstart + O&M of that year.
  5. Keep the defender as long as its marginal cost ≤ AE*C. Replace it in the first year when its marginal cost exceeds AE*C.
  6. Re-check every year with new estimates, because costs and technology change.

Example (numbers from Q6, MARR 10%): new printer AE*C = Rs 12,531.72 (ESL 3 years). Old printer AE*D = Rs 14,312.69. Its marginal costs for the next 4 years are 14,500; 14,300; 14,100; 14,900. All are above 12,531.72, so the old printer should be replaced now.

If the planning horizon is finite (for example, service needed for only 5 more years), we instead list all possible replacement plans (keep defender 0, 1, 2… years, then challenger) and choose the plan with the lowest PW of cost.
Under an infinite horizon, compare the defender’s minimum annual cost with the challenger’s minimum annual cost AE*C (at its ESL). Replace now if the defender costs more; otherwise keep it until its marginal yearly cost rises above AE*C.
7(b)Decisions under certainty, uncertainty and risk[4]
Explain decision making under certainty, uncertainty and risk.
Solution
The difference is how much we know about the future. Certainty: we know exactly what will happen. Risk: we know the possible outcomes and their chances (probabilities). Uncertainty: we know the possible outcomes but not their chances.
BasisCertaintyRiskUncertainty
Knowledge of futureComplete; one known outcomeSeveral outcomes; probabilities knownSeveral outcomes; probabilities not known
Tools usedPW, AW, IRR, B/C, break-even, linear programmingExpected value (EMV), variance, decision tree, simulationMaximax, Maximin, Laplace, Hurwicz, Minimax regret
ExampleFixed deposit at a fixed bank rateDam design with 30% chance of high flood (from past data)New product in a new market with no data

1. Decision under certainty

All data (costs, revenues, life, rate) are known exactly. Each option has only one result. We simply compute PW or AW and choose the best option. Most textbook problems assume certainty.

2. Decision under risk

Each option can have several results (states of nature), and we know the probability of each, usually from past records. We choose the option with the best expected monetary value: EMV = Σ (payoff × probability). Decision trees are used for multi-stage decisions.

3. Decision under uncertainty

We know the possible results but cannot give probabilities. The choice depends on the decision maker’s attitude:

  • Maximax (optimist): choose the best of the best payoffs.
  • Maximin (pessimist): choose the best of the worst payoffs.
  • Laplace: treat all states as equally likely and take the average.
  • Minimax regret: choose the option whose largest regret (missed profit) is the smallest.

1(a)Principles of engineering economics[4]
Explain the principles of engineering economics.
Solution
Engineering economics is the study of how an engineer chooses the best option (alternative) by comparing the money (costs and benefits) of each option over time. Seven basic rules, called the principles of engineering economics, guide every such decision.

The seven principles

  1. Develop the alternatives. A decision is a choice between two or more options. So first list all realistic options. “Do nothing” (keep the present system) is also an option.
  2. Focus on the differences. Only the differences in future costs and benefits matter. A cost that is the same for every option does not change the choice, so we can leave it out.
  3. Use a consistent viewpoint. Look at all cash flows from one fixed point of view (for example, the company or the owner). Do not mix the views of the owner, the customer and the government.
  4. Use a common unit of measure. Put as many results as possible into one unit, normally money (Rs). Then the options can be added and compared easily.
  5. Consider all relevant criteria. Money is not the only thing. Safety, environment, comfort, social and political effects should also be checked when they matter.
  6. Make risk and uncertainty clear. Future costs and incomes are only estimates. Say clearly what is uncertain and test how the result changes (for example by sensitivity analysis).
  7. Revisit your decisions. After the project runs, compare the real results with the estimates. This feedback helps to make better decisions next time.

Small example

A municipality must choose a water pump. A diesel pump costs Rs 2,00,000 and uses fuel of Rs 60,000 per year. An electric pump costs Rs 3,00,000 and uses power of Rs 30,000 per year. Both need the same operator salary of Rs 1,20,000 per year.

PrincipleHow it is used here
1Options: diesel pump, electric pump (and “repair the old pump”).
2Salary is the same for both, so ignore it. Compare Rs 1,00,000 extra first cost with a saving of Rs 30,000 per year.
3, 4View of the municipality; all amounts in Rs.
5The electric pump has no smoke (better for the environment).
6Check the result if the electricity price rises by 20%.
7After 2 years, compare the real power bill with the estimate.
The seven principles are: develop the alternatives, focus on the differences, use a consistent viewpoint, use a common unit of measure, consider all relevant criteria, make uncertainty clear, and revisit decisions.

Solution

Part 1: Inflation and its causes [4]

Inflation is a steady rise in the general price level of goods and services over time. Because prices go up, the same amount of money buys less. So inflation reduces the purchasing power of money. It is measured by a price index such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The yearly percentage rise is the inflation rate f.

Example: A bag of cement costs Rs 800 this year and Rs 840 next year. Inflation for cement = (840 − 800)/800 = 5% per year. Rs 840 next year buys only what Rs 800 buys today.

Causes of inflation:

Demand-pull inflation

Total demand for goods is more than the supply. People have more income or easy loans, so “too much money chases too few goods” and prices rise.

Cost-push inflation

The cost of making goods rises: higher wages, raw material, fuel and transport costs. Producers pass the higher cost on to buyers.

  • Increase in money supply: the central bank prints too much money or banks give too much credit.
  • Government deficit spending: the government spends more than it earns and borrows to cover the gap.
  • Imported inflation: prices of imported goods (for Nepal: petroleum, machinery) rise, or the rupee loses value.
  • Supply shortage: floods, poor harvest, strikes or border blockades reduce supply.
  • Higher indirect taxes: a rise in VAT, excise or customs duty raises selling prices.
  • Expectations: when people expect prices to rise, they buy early and ask for higher wages, which pushes prices up further.

4(a)Inconsistent ranking in IRR method[4]
Explain with suitable example how inconsistent ranking occurs in IRR method.
Solution
The IRR (internal rate of return) is the interest rate that makes PW = 0. When we compare mutually exclusive projects, the project with the higher IRR is not always the project with the higher PW at the MARR. When the IRR order and the PW order disagree, we call it inconsistent ranking. The PW method is correct because it measures the actual money gained.

Why it happens

  • Scale (size) difference: a small project can have a high IRR but give little total money. A big project with a lower IRR may give much more money.
  • Timing difference: one project returns money early, the other late. Their PW lines cross.
  • Different lives of the projects.
  • IRR assumes that cash received is reinvested at the IRR itself, while PW assumes reinvestment at the MARR.

Example (MARR = 10%)

7(a)Decision tree and its components[4]
Explain the Decision tree and its components.
Solution
A decision tree is a diagram that shows a decision problem step by step, like the branches of a tree. It shows the choices we can make, the chance events that may happen after each choice, their probabilities, and the final money results. It is used when decisions are made in sequence and under risk. We solve it from right to left (“rolling back”) using the expected monetary value (EMV).

Components

ComponentSymbolMeaning
Decision node□ squareA point where the decision maker chooses one option.
Decision branchline from □One option (alternative), often with its cost.
Chance node○ circleA point where the result depends on chance, not on us.
Chance branchline from ○One possible outcome with its probability. The probabilities from one chance node add up to 1.
Terminal (end) node△ triangleThe end of a path; shows the final payoff (profit or cost).
Payoff and EMVRs valueEMV at a chance node = Σ (probability × payoff). At a decision node, pick the branch with the best EMV.

Example

A company must build a large plant (cost Rs 50 lakh) or a small plant (cost Rs 20 lakh). Demand may be high (probability 0.6) or low (0.4).

  • Which plant?
    • Large plant (cost Rs 50 lakh)
      • High demand 0.6 → Rs 100 lakh
      • Low demand 0.4 → Rs 30 lakh
    • Small plant (cost Rs 20 lakh)
      • High demand 0.6 → Rs 50 lakh
      • Low demand 0.4 → Rs 40 lakh
EMV(large) = 0.6 × 100 + 0.4 × 30 − 50 = 72 − 50 = Rs 22 lakh
EMV(small) = 0.6 × 50 + 0.4 × 40 − 20 = 46 − 20 = Rs 26 lakh
Best decision: small plant (higher EMV)

Steps to use a decision tree

  1. Draw the tree from left to right: decision nodes, chance nodes and branches.
  2. Write the probabilities on the chance branches and the payoffs at the end nodes.
  3. Roll back from right to left: find EMV at each chance node.
  4. At each decision node, keep the best branch and cut (∥) the others.
A decision tree shows decisions, chance events, probabilities and payoffs as a tree. Its components are decision nodes (□), chance nodes (○), decision and chance branches, terminal nodes (△) with payoffs, and probabilities. It is solved by rolling back with EMV.

1Need of economics · principles of engineering economics[1+3]
Why do engineers need knowledge of economics in decision making process? Explain briefly about the principles of engineering economics.
Solution
An engineer can usually solve a problem in many ways that all work. But money, material and time are limited. Economics helps the engineer choose the way that gives the most value for the money. A design that works but costs too much is not a good design.

Part 1: Why engineers need economics [1]

  • Many technical choices: a bridge can be made of steel or concrete; a pump can be big or small. Economics tells which choice is best in money terms.
  • Limited (scarce) resources: capital, labour and material are limited, so they must go where they give the highest return.
  • Time value of money: Rs 1 today is worth more than Rs 1 after 5 years. Costs and benefits in different years can only be compared by using interest.
  • To justify projects: owners and managers decide in money language (PW, AW, IRR, B/C ratio, payback period).
  • Other decisions: replacing old machines, depreciation, tax, inflation and risk all need economic analysis.

Example: a road can be built in asphalt (cheap now, but repair every 5 years) or in concrete (costly now, repair every 20 years). Only an economic study of the whole-life cost tells which one is cheaper.

Part 2: Principles of engineering economics [3]

There are seven basic principles. Every economic study follows them.

  1. Develop the alternatives. A decision is a choice between options. List all workable options, including "do nothing".
  2. Focus on the differences. Only the costs and benefits that are different between the options matter. A cost that is the same for all options can be left out.
  3. Use a consistent viewpoint. Judge all options from one point of view (for example the company, or the public), not a mix of views.
  4. Use a common unit of measure. Put all results in one unit, usually money (Rs), so the options can be compared directly.
  5. Consider all relevant criteria. Besides money, also think about safety, environment, society and the goals of the owner.
  6. Make uncertainty explicit. Future values are only estimates. Show the risk openly, for example with sensitivity analysis.
  7. Revisit your decisions. After the project runs, compare the actual results with the estimates and learn for the next decision.
Engineers need economics to pick the option that is technically sound and also cheapest or most profitable, using limited resources and the time value of money. The seven principles: develop alternatives, focus on differences, consistent viewpoint, common unit of measure, all relevant criteria, make uncertainty explicit, revisit decisions.
2(a)Nominal vs effective interest rate[2+2]
Differentiate the nominal and effective interest with the support of; 10% bank's interest which compounds daily.
Solution
When a bank says "10% per year compounded daily", the 10% is only the stated (nominal) rate. Interest is added every day, and the next day you also earn interest on that interest. So at the end of the year you really earn a little more than 10%. That real yearly rate is the effective rate.

Part 1: Difference [2]

Nominal interest rate (r)
  • The rate stated per year, e.g. "10% per year compounded daily".
  • It does not include the effect of compounding inside the year.
  • r = rate per period × number of periods in a year (m).
  • Used for quoting rates (banks, loans).
  • Cannot be used directly in yearly formulas when interest is added more than once a year.
Effective interest rate (i)
  • The rate actually earned or paid in one year.
  • It includes compounding inside the year.
  • i = (1 + r/m)m − 1.
  • Used for calculations and for comparing loans or deposits.
  • Always greater than or equal to r. Equal only when m = 1 (yearly compounding).

Part 2: Example: 10% compounded daily [2]

Nominal rate r = 10% per year · interest is added daily, so m = 365 periods per year.
Rate per day = r / m   ·   ieff = (1 + r/m)m − 1
Rate per day = 0.10 / 365 = 0.000274 = 0.0274% per day
ieff = (1 + 0.10/365)365 − 1
ieff = 1.105156 − 1 = 0.105156 = 10.5156% per year

So the bank's 10% (nominal) is really about 10.52% (effective). If you deposit Rs 1,00,000 for one year:

Using the nominal rate only: 1,00,000 × 1.10 = Rs 1,10,000
Actual, with daily compounding: 1,00,000 × 1.105156 = Rs 1,10,515.6
Extra interest because of daily compounding ≈ Rs 515.6
Nominal rate = stated 10% (ignores compounding). Effective rate with daily compounding = 10.5156% per year. The effective rate is always higher than the nominal rate when interest is added more than once a year.

MARR is like the "pass mark" for an investment. If a project earns less than MARR, the money is better used somewhere else. In the AW method, we turn every cash flow into an equal yearly amount at MARR. If the yearly profit is more than the yearly cost of the capital, the project passes.

Part 1: Definition of MARR [1]

MARR (Minimum Attractive Rate of Return) is the lowest rate of return that a company or investor is willing to accept from a project. A project is accepted only if its rate of return is equal to or more than the MARR. It is also called the hurdle rate or cut-off rate. MARR is the interest rate used in PW, AW and FW calculations.

Part 2: Factors that decide MARR [2]

  • Cost of capital: the interest paid on borrowed money and the return wanted by owners (shareholders). MARR must be at least this.
  • Opportunity cost: the return of the best other investment that we give up by choosing this project.
  • Risk of the project: a risky project needs a higher MARR (an extra "risk premium").
  • Amount of money available: when capital is limited (capital rationing), only the best projects are taken, so MARR goes up.
  • Inflation: when prices rise fast, investors ask for a higher rate.
  • Taxes and government policy: tax rates, subsidies and the bank interest rates in the economy.
  • Business conditions: returns earned by other firms in the same industry, the state of the economy, and how long the project lasts.

Example: if money costs a firm 8% and it adds 4% for risk, its MARR = 8% + 4% = 12%.

4(a)Comparing alternatives with unequal lives[3]
Explain the techniques for comparing mutually exclusive alternatives having unequal useful lives.
Solution
Mutually exclusive means we can choose only one of the options. If one machine lasts 2 years and the other lasts 3 years, their PWs cannot be compared directly, because one gives service for longer. We must first make the service period the same for both. There are three common ways.

1. Repeatability assumption: LCM (common multiple) method

Take the study period equal to the least common multiple (LCM) of the lives. Assume each option is bought again and again with the same cash flows until the LCM ends. Then compare PW (or FW) over the LCM period.

Example: Machine A lasts 2 years and Machine B lasts 3 years. LCM = 6 years. A is bought 3 times and B is bought 2 times.

2. Annual worth (AW / EUAC) method

Under the same repeatability assumption, the AW of one life cycle is equal to the AW over the whole LCM period. So we simply find the AW (or equivalent uniform annual cost, EUAC) of one life of each option and compare. This is the easiest method, so it is used most in exams.

3. Co-terminated assumption: study period method

Choose a fixed study period (planning horizon), for example the shorter life or the number of years the service is really needed. For an option that lives longer than the study period, use its estimated market (salvage) value at the end of the study period. For an option that lives shorter, assume the service is rented, leased or contracted for the remaining years. Then compare PW over the same study period.

5(a)Replacement analysis · causes · two approaches[3]
What do you mean by replacement analysis? List out major cause of replacement. Distinguish between cash flow approach and opportunity cost approach in replacement.
Solution
Old machines get slower, break down more and cost more to run. New machines come with better technology. Replacement analysis answers: "Should we keep the old machine or buy a new one, and when?"

Meaning

Replacement analysis is the economic study to decide whether an existing asset should be kept or replaced now (or later) by a new asset. The existing asset is called the defender. The new asset that may replace it is called the challenger. We compare their equivalent annual costs (EUAC) over the right period and choose the cheaper one.

Major causes of replacement

  • Physical impairment (wear and tear): the asset deteriorates, so repair and running costs rise and output falls.
  • Obsolescence: a new, better or cheaper technology is available.
  • Inadequacy: the asset cannot meet the new demand (capacity is too small or too big).
  • Rising operating and maintenance costs as the asset gets older.
  • Other reasons: leasing or renting becomes cheaper; new safety or pollution rules.

Cash flow approach vs opportunity cost approach

Cash flow approach
  • The money from selling the defender is treated as a cash inflow for the challenger.
  • Challenger's first cost = price − market value of defender.
  • Defender's first cost = 0 (we already own it).
  • Correct only when both assets have the same remaining life.
Opportunity cost approach (outsider view)
  • The defender's current market value is treated as the investment needed to keep the defender (money we give up by not selling it).
  • Challenger's first cost = its full price.
  • Defender's first cost = its current market value.
  • Works for equal and unequal lives; it is the recommended method.

Example: defender market value Rs 5,000, challenger price Rs 7,500. Cash flow approach: defender 0, challenger 7,500 − 5,000 = Rs 2,500. Opportunity cost approach: defender Rs 5,000, challenger Rs 7,500. In both, the difference is Rs 2,500, so with equal lives both give the same decision (see Q5(b)).

Replacement analysis decides between keeping the defender or buying the challenger. Main causes: wear and tear, obsolescence, inadequacy, rising O&M cost. The cash flow approach credits the defender's sale value to the challenger; the opportunity cost approach charges the defender's market value as its own investment.

6(a)Methods of describing risk · decision tree[1+3]
List out methods of describing risk. Explain Decision tree analysis.
Solution
Risk means the future result is not certain: the cash flows may turn out higher or lower than we estimate. The methods below show how much the result can change, so that the decision is made with open eyes.

Part 1: Methods of describing project risk [1]

  • Sensitivity analysis: change one input at a time and see how PW or IRR changes.
  • Break-even analysis: find the value of an input at which the project just breaks even (PW = 0).
  • Scenario analysis: study optimistic, most likely and pessimistic cases.
  • Probability approach: expected value, variance and standard deviation of PW.
  • Decision tree analysis for decisions taken in stages.
  • Monte Carlo simulation and the risk-adjusted MARR are also used.

Part 2: Decision tree analysis [3]

A decision tree is a diagram that shows a decision, the possible chance events after it, their probabilities and the final money results. It is drawn from left to right like the branches of a tree. It is very useful when decisions are taken one after another (sequential decisions).

Symbols: a square ■ = decision node (we choose); a circle ● = chance node (nature decides, with probabilities); a triangle ▶ at the end of a branch = outcome or end node (payoff in Rs).

  1. Draw the tree from left to right: decision nodes, then chance nodes, then outcomes.
  2. Write the probability of each chance branch (they must add up to 1) and the payoff at each end.
  3. Work backwards from right to left ("roll back"). At each chance node find the expected monetary value: EMV = Σ (probability × payoff).
  4. At each decision node, choose the branch with the best EMV (highest profit or lowest cost) and cut the other branches.
  5. The branches that remain show the best decision path.

Small example: a firm can launch a new product at a cost of Rs 2,00,000. Demand may be high (probability 0.6, return Rs 5,00,000) or low (probability 0.4, return Rs 1,00,000). Or it can do nothing (Rs 0).

  • Launch the product?
    • Launch (cost Rs 2,00,000) · EMV = Rs 1,40,000
      • High demand, p = 0.6 → Rs 5,00,000
      • Low demand, p = 0.4 → Rs 1,00,000
    • Do not launch → Rs 0
EMV(Launch) = 0.6 × 5,00,000 + 0.4 × 1,00,000 − 2,00,000
EMV(Launch) = 3,40,000 − 2,00,000 = Rs 1,40,000 > 0
Best decision: Launch

Advantages: it gives a clear picture of a complex problem, it handles decisions in many stages, and it shows the expected value of each choice. Limitation: the tree becomes very large when there are many choices, and the result depends on the probability estimates.

Methods of describing risk: sensitivity, break-even, scenario analysis, probability (expected value and variance), decision tree, simulation. A decision tree shows decisions, chance events, probabilities and payoffs; we roll back using EMV and choose the branch with the best expected value.

1Economics · scope of engineering economics[1+3]
Define economics. Briefly explain the scope of engineering economics with appropriate example.
Solution
Resources like money, land, labour and time are always limited, but our wants are many. Economics is about choosing well with limited resources. Engineering economics uses the same idea for engineering work: before we build or buy anything, we check whether it is worth the money.

Definition of economics [1]

Economics is the social science that studies how people, firms and governments use scarce (limited) resources, which have other possible uses, to satisfy their unlimited wants. Lionel Robbins said: "Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."

Engineering economics is the use of economic principles to choose the best engineering option. It compares the costs and benefits of each option over time, using interest (the time value of money).

Scope of engineering economics [3]

The scope means the areas where engineering economics is used. The main areas are:

AreaWhat it answersExample
1. Time value of moneyWhat is a future amount worth today?Rs 1 lakh after 5 years is worth less than Rs 1 lakh today.
2. Comparing alternativesWhich option is cheapest or most profitable? (PW, AW, FW, IRR, B/C)Choosing between two pumps, two road designs.
3. Cost estimation and break-evenWhat will it cost? How many units must we sell to avoid loss?Minimum bricks a kiln must sell per year.
4. Depreciation and taxHow much value does an asset lose? What is the after-tax profit?Writing off a truck over 5 years.
5. Replacement analysisWhen should old equipment be replaced?Keep the old excavator or buy a new one?
6. Risk and uncertaintyWhat if the estimates are wrong?Sensitivity of a hydropower project to river flow.
7. Public projectsDo benefits to society exceed costs?B/C ratio of a highway or irrigation canal.
8. Inflation and financingHow do rising prices and loans change the decision?Planning a 10-year project when prices rise 6% a year.

Example with numbers

A small hydropower company must choose a turbine for 10 years. Interest rate (MARR) = 10%.

Turbine A: cost Rs 40 lakh, running cost Rs 3 lakh per year · Turbine B: cost Rs 50 lakh, running cost Rs 1.2 lakh per year · (A/P, 10%, 10) = 0.1627
AW cost of A = 40 × 0.1627 + 3 = Rs 9.51 lakh per year
AW cost of B = 50 × 0.1627 + 1.2 = Rs 9.34 lakh per year

Turbine B costs more at the start, but it is cheaper per year overall. Without engineering economics, we might wrongly pick A just because its first cost is lower.

Economics is the study of using scarce resources to meet unlimited wants. Engineering economics applies it to engineering choices: time value of money, comparing alternatives, cost and break-even, depreciation and tax, replacement, risk, public projects and inflation.
2(a)Time value of money · nominal vs effective rate[1+2+2]
What are the factors to be considered in calculating the time value of money? Distinguish between nominal and effective interest with suitable example. Bank 'A' offers 6.25% interest that compounds daily and Bank 'B' offers 6.4% interest that compounds yearly, which bank do you prefer and why?
Solution
Money today is worth more than the same money later, because today’s money can earn interest. To compare two banks fairly, we must change both rates into the effective yearly rate, which is the interest you really get in one year.

Part 1: Factors in the time value of money [1]

  1. Amount of money (principal P): the sum that is invested or borrowed.
  2. Interest rate (i): the price paid for using money, in % per period.
  3. Time (n): how long the money is invested or borrowed.
  4. Compounding frequency (m): how often interest is added (yearly, monthly, daily).
  5. Timing of cash flows: whether money comes at the start or end of a period. (Inflation and risk also affect the rate we use.)

Part 2: Nominal vs effective interest rate [2]

Nominal rate (r)

The yearly rate that is stated or quoted, without counting compounding inside the year.

Written as "12% per year compounded monthly".

Formula: r = (rate per period) × m.

It is lower than (or equal to) the effective rate.

Effective rate (ie)

The real yearly rate that you earn or pay after counting all the compounding in the year.

Formula: ie = (1 + r/m)m − 1.

Nominal and effective are equal only when interest is compounded once a year.

It is the correct rate for comparing offers.

Example: 12% per year compounded monthly means 1% each month. Rs 100 grows to 100 × (1.01)12 = Rs 112.68 in one year. So the nominal rate is 12%, but the effective rate is 12.68%.

Part 3: Which bank? [2]

Bank A: r = 6.25% per year, compounded daily (m = 365) · Bank B: 6.4% per year, compounded yearly (m = 1)
ie = (1 + r/m)m − 1
Bank A: ie = (1 + 0.0625/365)365 − 1
= (1.000171233)365 − 1 = 0.064489 = 6.449%
Bank B: ie = (1 + 0.064/1)1 − 1 = 6.400%

Check with Rs 1,00,000 kept for one year: Bank A gives Rs 1,06,448.88 and Bank B gives Rs 1,06,400.00. Bank A gives Rs 48.88 more.

The chart shows that the more often interest is added, the higher the effective rate. With daily compounding, 6.25% becomes 6.449%, which passes Bank B’s 6.40%.

As a depositor, choose Bank A: its effective rate is 6.449%, which is higher than Bank B’s 6.40%. (If you were borrowing money, Bank B would be cheaper.)

3(a)Payback period · advantages and limitations[4]
Write advantages and limitations of payback period method with suitable example.
Solution
The payback period is the time a project needs to get back its first investment from its net cash inflows. A shorter payback is seen as better. It answers "how fast do I get my money back?", but not "how much do I earn in total?".
Simple payback: smallest n where Σ (net cash flow, years 0 to n) ≥ 0
Discounted payback: smallest n where Σ CFk(P/F, i, k) ≥ 0

Example

Two projects each cost Rs 10,000. MARR = 10%.

Year012345PaybackPW at 10%
Project X−10,0005,0005,0000002 years−1,322
Project Y−10,0004,0003,0003,0008,0008,0003 years+8,801

By payback, X looks better (2 years against 3 years). But X only returns the money and earns no interest, so its PW is negative. Y earns a lot after year 3, so Y is much better. This one example shows both the use and the weakness of the method.

Advantages

1. Very simple to calculate and easy to understand, even for non-engineers.

2. Gives a quick measure of liquidity: how fast the cash comes back.

3. Acts as a rough measure of risk: money that comes back fast is exposed to less uncertainty.

4. Useful for screening many projects quickly, and for firms that are short of cash or in fast-changing fields (like electronics).

Limitations

1. Ignores all cash flows after the payback period (Project Y above).

2. Simple payback ignores the time value of money (the discounted payback fixes this only partly).

3. Does not measure profitability; a short payback does not mean a high return.

4. The maximum acceptable payback is chosen by judgement, not by theory. It can wrongly rank mutually exclusive projects.

Payback is simple, shows liquidity and risk, and is good for quick screening. But it ignores cash flows after payback and (in simple form) the time value of money, so it should be used only as a supporting method beside PW, AW or IRR.

4(a)Project types · capitalized worth · incremental analysis[2+2+2]
Define mutually exclusive, independent and contingent projects. What are the conditions to apply capitalized worth method? Why do we need incremental analysis?
Solution
Before comparing projects, we must know how they relate to each other: can we pick many, only one, or does one need another? Capitalized worth is a special PW for projects that last "forever". Incremental analysis makes sure we pick the right one among mutually exclusive projects.

Part 1: Types of projects [2]

TypeMeaningExample
Mutually exclusiveChoosing one project rules out all the others. At most one can be selected.For one site: build a hotel, OR a hospital, OR a school.
IndependentChoosing one has no effect on the others. We can choose any number, as long as each is profitable and money is available.A company buying a new truck and also installing solar panels.
ContingentA project that can be chosen only if another project (its "parent") is chosen first.An air-conditioning system for a building can be chosen only if the building is built.

Part 2: Conditions to apply the capitalized worth method [2]

Capitalized worth (CW) is the present worth of all cash flows when the life is infinite. For an equal yearly amount A forever, CW = A / i.

  1. The project has a very long or permanent life (usually more than 40–50 years), e.g. dams, bridges, roads, canals, tunnels, railways, endowment funds.
  2. The cash flows repeat in a regular pattern forever (a uniform yearly amount, or a cost that repeats every k years).
  3. The interest rate stays the same over the whole (infinite) period.
  4. It is used to compare alternatives assumed to give service forever, or when the cost of perpetual service is needed (e.g. how much to deposit now to pay for maintenance forever).

Part 3: Why do we need incremental analysis? [2]

When projects are mutually exclusive, ranking by each project’s own IRR, ERR or B/C ratio can pick the wrong project. A high percentage on a small investment may give less total wealth than a slightly lower percentage on a bigger investment. Incremental analysis asks: "Does the extra money in the bigger project earn at least the MARR?" If yes, choose the bigger project. This always agrees with the PW method (largest PW).

Example (MARR = 10%, one-year projects):

Mutually exclusive: pick at most one. Independent: pick any that pass. Contingent: depends on a parent project. Capitalized worth is used for very long or perpetual lives with repeating cash flows and a constant rate. Incremental analysis is needed so that IRR, ERR or B/C ranking of mutually exclusive projects gives the same correct choice as PW.

5(b)Defender, challenger · cash flow vs opportunity cost approach[3+1]
Define defender, challenger and explain cashflow approach and opportunity cost approach of comparing .Why replacement of existing equipment is necessary?
Solution
In a replacement study we compare the asset we already own with a new one. The difficulty is how to treat the money we would get by selling the old asset. The two approaches put this money in different places, but they give the same decision when used correctly.

Defender and challenger

Defender: the existing (old) asset that is now in use and is being considered for replacement. Example: the old bakery oven in Q5(a).

Challenger: the new asset (best available option) that is being considered to replace the defender. Example: the new Rs 80,000 oven.

Two approaches for comparing them

Cash flow approach

We look at the actual cash that moves. If we buy the challenger, we sell the defender, so the defender’s sale money (market value) is subtracted from the challenger’s first cost.

The defender then has no first cost; only its future operating costs and salvage are counted.

Example (Q5(a)): challenger net first cost = 80,000 − 20,000 = Rs 60,000; defender first cost = 0.

Works well only when the defender’s remaining life equals the challenger’s life. With unequal lives the sale money gets spread over the wrong number of years.

Opportunity cost approach (outsider view)

If we keep the defender, we give up the chance to sell it. So its current market value is treated as the defender’s first cost (an opportunity cost).

The challenger’s first cost is its full price.

Example (Q5(a)): defender first cost = Rs 20,000; challenger first cost = Rs 80,000.

This is the preferred method, because it works for unequal lives and makes the comparison of annual costs (EUAC) easy.

In both approaches, the original purchase price and the book value of the defender are sunk costs (money already spent). They are ignored. Only the market value today and future cash flows matter.

Why is replacement necessary? [1]

  1. Physical wear (deterioration): old equipment works less well and needs more repair, so operating and maintenance costs rise.
  2. Obsolescence: new technology does the job faster, cheaper or with better quality.
  3. Inadequacy: the old asset cannot meet higher demand or new requirements (capacity, safety, environmental rules).
  4. Economics: a new asset may simply have a lower annual cost, which saves money.
The defender is the existing asset; the challenger is its possible replacement. The cash flow approach subtracts the defender’s sale value from the challenger’s cost. The opportunity cost approach uses the defender’s market value as its first cost (preferred). Replacement is needed because of wear, obsolescence, inadequacy and to cut costs.

8Inflation · types, effects, average rate[1+2+2]
Distinguish between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. What are the effects of inflation in an economy? Suppose that inflation for the first and second year is 5% and 8% respectively. Calculate the average inflation rate for the two years if the base price is Rs. 100.
Solution
Inflation is a steady rise in the general price level, so the same money buys less over time. Inflation compounds like interest: the second year’s rise is applied to the already higher price. So the average rate is a geometric mean, not a simple average.

Part 1: Demand-pull vs cost-push inflation [1]

Demand-pull inflation

Prices rise because total demand is more than total supply ("too much money chasing too few goods").

Causes: more money supply, easy loans, high government spending, rising incomes or remittance.

Example: prices of land and houses rise when many buyers have extra money.

Cost-push inflation

Prices rise because the cost of production goes up, and producers pass it on to buyers.

Causes: higher wages, costlier raw materials, fuel price hikes, higher taxes, a weak currency.

Example: a rise in diesel price raises transport fares and food prices.

Part 2: Effects of inflation on an economy [2]

  1. Lower purchasing power: the same income buys fewer goods. People on fixed incomes (pensioners, salaried staff) suffer most.
  2. Debtors gain, creditors lose: loans are repaid with money that is worth less.
  3. Savings lose value: if the bank interest rate is below inflation, the real return is negative, so people save less.
  4. Uncertainty in investment: future costs and revenues are hard to estimate, so projects look riskier and the market interest rate (MARR) goes up.
  5. Unequal income distribution: businesses and owners of real assets may gain, while workers lose.
  6. Trade effects: local goods become costlier than foreign goods, so exports fall and imports rise.

Part 3: Average inflation rate [2]

Base price = Rs 100 · f1 = 5% · f2 = 8%
Price after year 1 = 100 × 1.05 = Rs 105.00

Average f = [(1 + f1)(1 + f2)]1/2 − 1 (geometric mean)

(1 + f)2 = 1.05 × 1.08 = 1.1340

An engineer can usually solve a problem in many technical ways. But money, material and time are limited. Engineering economics helps the engineer pick the way that gives the most value for the money. A design that works but costs too much is not a good design.

Meaning

Engineering economics is the use of economic ideas to make engineering decisions. It compares different options using their costs, benefits and the time value of money (the idea that Rs 100 today is worth more than Rs 100 next year, because today’s money can earn interest).

Why it is important for engineers

  1. Choosing the best alternative. There are many designs, machines, materials and methods. Economics tells us which one is cheapest or most profitable over its whole life, not just at the start.
  2. Time value of money. Costs and benefits come in different years. Tools like PW, AW, FW, IRR and B/C ratio let us compare them fairly.
  3. Best use of scarce resources. Capital, labour, energy and materials are limited, especially in a developing country like Nepal. Economic analysis avoids waste.
  4. Project feasibility. Before a road, hydropower plant or factory is built, engineers must show that it is worth doing (for example B/C ratio ≥ 1 for public projects, IRR ≥ MARR for private projects).
  5. Cost control and pricing. Break-even analysis, budgeting and cost estimation help engineers keep a project within budget and set the right price.
  6. Replacement, depreciation and tax. Engineers decide when to replace old equipment and how depreciation and tax change the real profit.
  7. Handling risk. Sensitivity and break-even analysis show what happens if costs or sales change.
  8. Talking with management. Owners and banks understand money. Economic analysis puts technical ideas into money terms.

2(a)Simple vs compound interest · nominal vs effective rate · tripling time[1+2+2]
Differentiate between simple and compound interest. What is the relation between nominal and effective interest rate? How long will it take for Rs 25,000 to triple itself, if the interest rate is 8% per year?
Solution
Interest is the rent paid for using money. In simple interest, interest is earned only on the original amount. In compound interest, interest also earns interest. That is why compound money grows faster.

Part 1: Simple vs compound interest [1]

Simple interest

Interest is charged only on the principal (original amount).

I = P × i × N,   F = P(1 + iN)

Interest is the same every year. Money grows in a straight line.

Rs 10,000 at 10% for 3 years: F = 10,000 × 1.30 = Rs 13,000

Compound interest

Interest is charged on the principal plus the interest already added.

F = P(1 + i)N

Interest grows every year. Money grows faster and faster (exponential).

Part 2: Nominal and effective interest rate [2]

Nominal rate (r): the yearly rate that is written in the agreement, for example "12% per year compounded monthly". It does not show the effect of compounding inside the year. r = (rate per period) × (number of periods per year).

Effective rate (i): the actual rate earned in one year after the compounding inside the year is counted.

i = (1 + r/m)m − 1    (m = number of compounding periods per year)
Continuous compounding (m → ∞): i = er − 1
  • If m = 1 (yearly compounding), effective rate = nominal rate.
  • If m > 1, effective rate is more than nominal rate. More compounding gives a higher effective rate, up to the limit er − 1.
Example: r = 12% per year compounded monthly (m = 12)
i = (1 + 0.12/12)12 − 1 = (1.01)12 − 1 = 0.1268 = 12.68%
Continuous: i = e0.12 − 1 = 12.75%

4(c)Mutually exclusive, independent and contingent projects[4]
Define mutually exclusive, independent and contingent projects, Give examples.
Solution
Before we compare projects, we must know how they are related. Does choosing one stop us from choosing another? Can we choose all of them? Does one need another to exist first? The answer decides which method of comparison we use.

1. Mutually exclusive projects

Projects where choosing one rules out all the others. Only one can be selected, usually because they do the same job or use the same site.

Example: To cross a river at one place, a city can build a steel bridge, an RCC bridge or a suspension bridge. Only one bridge will be built. Another example: buying Machine A or Machine B for the same job.

With n mutually exclusive projects, there are n + 1 choices (including "do nothing"). They are compared by PW/AW, or by incremental IRR or B/C analysis.

2. Independent projects

Projects where accepting or rejecting one does not affect the others. Any number of them can be chosen, as long as there is enough money.

Example: A company is thinking about (i) a new computer lab, (ii) a rooftop solar plant and (iii) a delivery van. Each is judged on its own (accept if PW ≥ 0, IRR ≥ MARR or B/C ≥ 1).

With n independent projects there are 2n possible combinations. If the budget is limited, we choose the best combination within the budget.

3. Contingent projects

A project that can be accepted only if another project is accepted first. Its acceptance depends on (is "contingent on") another project.

Example: A transmission line from a site is contingent on building the hydropower plant there. Buying air-conditioners for a building is contingent on constructing the building. A software licence is contingent on buying the computers.

The main project and its contingent project are usually analysed as one combined option (e.g. "Plant" and "Plant + Line").

5(b)Replacement analysis steps · finite and infinite horizon[4]
Write step by step process of replacement analysis when the planning horizon is finite and infinite.
Solution
Replacement analysis decides when an old asset (the defender) should be replaced by a new one (the challenger). The planning horizon (study period) is how far into the future we look. If the service is needed for a very long time, the horizon is infinite. If it is needed only for a fixed time (say 5 years), it is finite.

A. Infinite planning horizon

Here we assume the challenger can be repeated forever with the same costs, so we compare annual costs.

  1. Economic service life of the challenger: find EUAC for N = 1, 2, 3, … years. The N with the lowest EUAC is its economic life. Call this lowest value EUAC*C.
  2. Economic service life of the defender: use the opportunity cost approach (today’s market value is its "investment"). Find EUAC for N = 1, 2, … and the lowest value EUAC*D.
  3. Compare: if EUAC*D > EUAC*C, replace the defender now. If EUAC*D ≤ EUAC*C, keep the defender for now.
  4. Find the replacement year: if we keep it, keep it as long as its marginal cost for the next year (loss in value + interest + operating cost) is less than EUAC*C. Replace it in the first year in which its marginal cost is higher.
  5. Review every year with new data, because new challengers and new cost estimates appear.

B. Finite planning horizon

Here the service is needed only for T years, so we compare replacement strategies over exactly T years.

  1. Fix the horizon T (e.g. 5 years) and collect data: market values, O&M costs and salvage values of defender and challenger for each year.
  2. List all possible strategies that cover exactly T years, e.g. keep the defender 0, 1, 2, … years, then use the challenger for the remaining years.
  3. Draw the cash flows of each strategy year by year. Include the market value of any asset that is still useful at year T as a salvage (inflow).
  4. Find PW (or AW) of cost of each strategy at MARR over the T years.
  5. Choose the strategy with the lowest PW of cost. This tells us when to replace.
Example of finite-horizon strategies for T = 4 years, defender can last 2 more years, challenger life 4 years: (D0, C4), (D1, C3), (D2, C2). Find PW of cost for each and pick the lowest.

Finite horizon

List every replacement strategy that fits T years. Compare total PW of cost. Choose the lowest.

7(b)Depreciation · causes · after-tax analysis[1+1+2]
What do you mean by depreciation? Explain the causes for it. Elaborate about after tax economic analysis.
Solution
Machines and buildings lose value as time passes. Depreciation spreads the cost of an asset over the years it is used. It is not a cash payment, but it lowers taxable income, so it lowers the tax we pay. That is why it matters in after-tax analysis.

1. Meaning of depreciation [1]

Depreciation is the decrease in the value of a physical asset with time and use. In accounting, it is the systematic sharing of the asset’s cost (first cost − salvage value) over its useful life as a yearly expense. Example: a machine bought for Rs 1,00,000 with salvage Rs 20,000 after 8 years loses Rs 80,000; by the straight-line method that is Rs 10,000 per year.

2. Causes of depreciation [1]

  • Physical deterioration (wear and tear): parts wear out with use, rust and weather.
  • Functional / technological obsolescence: a newer, better or cheaper model comes to the market (e.g. old computers).
  • Inadequacy: the asset can no longer meet the higher demand.
  • Passage of time / decay: some assets lose value just with age, even if unused; leases and patents expire.
  • Accidents and depletion: damage by accident; natural resources (mines, forests) get used up.

3. After-tax economic analysis [2]

Companies pay income tax on profit. Tax is a real cash outflow, so a proper study must use after-tax cash flow (ATCF) and an after-tax MARR. Depreciation enters here: it is deducted before tax is calculated, so it reduces tax (this saving is called the depreciation tax shield = tax rate × depreciation).

  1. Find the before-tax cash flow (BTCF) = revenue − operating costs, for each year.
  2. Find the depreciation for each year (SL, MACRS, etc.).
  3. Taxable income = BTCF − depreciation (− interest on loans, if any).
  4. Income tax = tax rate × taxable income.
  5. ATCF = BTCF − income tax. (Add the after-tax salvage at the end.)
  6. Find PW, AW or IRR of the ATCF at the after-tax MARR (roughly before-tax MARR × (1 − tax rate)) and decide.

1Principles of engineering economics[2+2]
"Use the consistent view point" and "Make uncertainty explicit". Explain these two principles of engineering economics.
Solution
Engineering economics has a few basic rules (principles) that make every money decision fair and correct. Two of them are: look at the problem from one fixed point of view, and openly show that future numbers are not certain.

(i) Use a consistent viewpoint [2]

A viewpoint (perspective) means "whose gain and whose loss are we counting?" It may be the company (owner), the customer, the government, or the whole society. The principle says:

  • Decide the viewpoint at the start of the study.
  • Use the same viewpoint for every alternative and every cash flow until the end.
  • Usually the viewpoint of the owner of the firm is used for private projects. For public projects the viewpoint of the whole society is used.
  • If we mix viewpoints, some costs are counted twice or missed. Then the result is wrong.

Example: A company buys a machine. From the company's viewpoint, income tax of Rs 10,000 is a cost. From the government's viewpoint, the same Rs 10,000 is income. In one study we must count it only as the company sees it (a cost). We must not also add it as a benefit.

(ii) Make uncertainty explicit [2]

All economic studies use estimates of the future: revenue, cost, life, salvage value, interest rate. These estimates can be wrong. Uncertainty means we do not know the exact future value. The principle says we must not hide this. We must show it clearly (explicitly) in the analysis:

  • Give a range of values (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic), not only one number.
  • Use sensitivity analysis (change one estimate and see how the answer changes) and break-even analysis.
  • Use probability, expected value and decision trees when chances are known.

Example: A project has expected revenue Rs 8,000 per year, but it may be anywhere from Rs 6,000 to Rs 10,000. The analyst shows the PW for all three values. If PW stays positive even at Rs 6,000, the decision maker can accept with confidence. If not, he knows the risk before deciding.

Consistent viewpoint

Fix whose costs and benefits are counted. Keep it the same for all alternatives.

Make uncertainty explicit

Show how sure the estimates are. Use ranges, sensitivity, break-even and probability.

A consistent viewpoint keeps the counting correct: one owner, the same for everything. Making uncertainty explicit keeps the decision honest: the risk in future estimates is shown openly before the choice is made.

Money today can be invested and can earn interest. So Rs 100 today is worth more than Rs 100 next year. This is the base of all engineering economics.

Part 1: Time value of money [1]

The time value of money means that the value of money changes with time. A rupee received today is worth more than a rupee received in the future. Reasons:

  • Earning power: money today can be invested to earn interest.
  • Inflation: prices rise, so future money buys less.
  • Risk: future money may not be received at all.

Example: At 10% interest, Rs 100 today becomes Rs 110 after one year. So Rs 100 now and Rs 110 after one year are equivalent (equal in value).

Interest is often added (compounded) more than once a year: every month, every quarter, etc.

  • Nominal rate (r): the rate written on paper, per year. It ignores compounding inside the year. r = (rate per period) × (number of periods per year). Example: 1% per month = 12% per year nominal.
  • Effective rate (i): the real rate earned in one year, after compounding inside the year is included.
i = (1 + r/m)m − 1    (m = compounding periods per year)   ·   continuous: i = er − 1
Nominal rate
  • Stated (quoted) annual rate.

Effective rate
  • Actual annual rate earned or paid.
  • Includes interest on interest within the year.
  • i = (1 + r/m)m − 1.
  • Always ≥ nominal rate. Equal only when m = 1.

How it is performed

  1. Arrange the alternatives in increasing order of investment.
  2. Check the lowest-cost one against "do nothing" (its IRR ≥ MARR?). It becomes the current best.
  3. Find the increment: next alternative minus current best (extra cost, extra income).
  4. Find ΔIRR (or ΔB/C, ΔERR) of the increment. If ΔIRR ≥ MARR (or ΔB/C ≥ 1), the costlier one becomes the new best. If not, keep the current best.
  5. Repeat until all alternatives are tested. The last "best" is chosen.
Incremental analysis is needed because the highest IRR or B/C does not always mean the most total benefit. We test each extra investment against MARR. In the example, ΔIRR = 15.24% > 12%, so we choose B, the same answer as the PW method.

5(a)Need for replacement · finite planning horizon[2+2]
Why replacement analysis is necessary in economic analysis? How do you make replacement analysis when planning horizon in finite?
Solution
The machine we already have is the defender. The new machine that may replace it is the challenger. Replacement analysis tells us whether to replace and when to replace, so that the service is given at the lowest cost.

Part 1: Why replacement analysis is necessary [2]

  • Physical deterioration: an old asset needs more repair, more fuel and gives lower output. Its yearly cost keeps rising.
  • Obsolescence: new technology makes better and cheaper machines, so the old one becomes uneconomical even if it still works.
  • Inadequacy: the old asset cannot meet the new demand or capacity.
  • Changing needs and rules: new safety or environmental rules, or a new product, may need new equipment.
  • Economic reason: replacing too early wastes money, and replacing too late also wastes money (high O&M). The analysis finds the best time. It treats the old asset’s current market value as its investment (opportunity cost) and ignores sunk cost.

Part 2: Replacement analysis with a finite planning horizon [2]

A finite planning horizon means the service is needed only for a fixed number of years (for example 5 years). Then we cannot assume the challenger is repeated forever. We use the enumeration (strategy) method:

  1. Fix the planning horizon (e.g. 5 years) and the MARR.
  2. Estimate year-by-year O&M and market (salvage) values for the defender and the challenger. Find AEC for each possible life.
  3. List all possible strategies. Example: keep the defender 0, 1, 2 or 3 years, then use the challenger for the rest of the horizon.
  4. For each strategy, draw the cash flows over the full horizon (investment, O&M, salvage at disposal and at the end) and find its PW (or AEC) of cost at MARR.
  5. Choose the strategy with the lowest PW of cost. Its first step tells us what to do now.

Example of strategies (horizon 5 years, defender can last 3 more years, challenger life up to 5 years):

The strategy with the smallest PW of cost is chosen. Example: if strategy 2 is cheapest, we keep the old machine for one more year and then buy the new one.

Replacement analysis is needed because assets wear out, become old-fashioned or too small. It finds the cheapest time to replace. For a finite horizon, list every keep-then-replace strategy that covers the horizon, find the PW of cost of each at MARR, and choose the minimum.

8Actual vs constant dollars · average inflation[1+3]
What is Actual and Constant Dollar? The cost of apple per kg is Rs 150. The Cost increases to 6%, 8% and 9% per kg in first year, second year and third year respectively. Determine the average inflation rate for 3 years.
Solution
Inflation means prices go up over time, so the same money buys less. To compare money of different years we must say whether it is in "actual" or "constant" terms. The average inflation over several years with different yearly rates is a geometric mean, not a simple average.

Part 1: Actual and constant dollars [1]

Actual (then-current) dollars

Money amounts as they will really be paid or received in that year, including inflation. Example: an apple costs Rs 159 next year. That Rs 159 is in actual rupees.

Constant (real) dollars

Money amounts shown in the buying power of a base year, with inflation removed. Example: Rs 159 next year at 6% inflation = 159/1.06 = Rs 150 in today’s (base-year) rupees.

Constant amount = Actual amount / (1 + f)n

Part 2: Average inflation rate [3]

Base price = Rs 150 per kg · f1 = 6%, f2 = 8%, f3 = 9% (the price increases by these rates each year)

Step 1: Price at the end of each year.

Year 1: 150 × 1.06 = Rs 159.00
Year 2: 159 × 1.08 = Rs 171.72
Year 3: 171.72 × 1.09 = Rs 187.17

Step 2: The average rate f must give the same final price when applied every year.

(1 + f)3 = (1 + f1)(1 + f2)(1 + f3)  ⇒  f = [(1.06)(1.08)(1.09)]1/3 − 1
(1.06)(1.08)(1.09) = 1.247832
f = (1.247832)1/3 − 1 = 1.0766 − 1

The simple average (6 + 8 + 9)/3 = 7.67% is slightly wrong, because inflation compounds year on year. Always use the geometric mean.

Uniform series: P/A and A/P

P = A [(1+i)N − 1] / [i(1+i)N] → (P/A, i, N)

Uniform series: F/A and A/F

F = A [(1+i)N − 1] / i → (F/A, i, N)
A = F i / [(1+i)N − 1] → (A/F, i, N) (sinking fund factor)
F is at the same time as the last A.

Perpetuity (money forever)

3Gradient series
Amounts that grow by a fixed rupee amount (arithmetic) or a fixed % (geometric)
Formulas

Arithmetic gradient (grows by G each year)

(P/G, i, N) = [(1+i)N − 1 − Ni] / [i2(1+i)N]
(A/G, i, N) = 1/i − N / [(1+i)N − 1]
P = A1(P/A, i, N) + G(P/G, i, N)

Geometric gradient (grows by g% each year)

If i ≠ g: P = A1 [1 − (1+g)N(1+i)−N] / (i − g)
If i = g: P = N·A1 / (1 + i)

5Comparing alternatives
Incremental analysis and unequal lives
Steps
  1. Remove any project that fails on its own (IRR < MARR, or B/C < 1).
  2. Order the rest from the lowest to the highest investment.
  3. The cheapest one is the "current best" (defender).
  4. Find the extra cash flow: (next project) − (current best).
  5. If the extra investment earns ≥ MARR (ΔIRR ≥ MARR or ΔB/C ≥ 1), the bigger project becomes the current best.
  6. Repeat until all are tested. The last "current best" is chosen.
Repeatability assumption

Each project can be repeated with the same cash flows. Compare by AW (easy), or use the LCM of the lives as the study period.

Coterminated assumption

Use a fixed study period. Cut the longer project (use its market value at the end), or extend the shorter one (lease or contract) to fill the gap.

6Replacement analysis
Defender vs challenger, economic service life
Formulas
Defender

The old machine you have now. Its "investment" is its current market value (the money you give up by keeping it).

Challenger

The new machine you might buy.

Cash flow approach

The sale price of the old machine is treated as cash coming in for the challenger (it reduces the challenger’s first cost).

Opportunity cost approach

The sale price of the old machine is treated as the investment (first cost) of the defender. This is the usual textbook method.

EUAC(N) = CR(N) + equivalent O&M
CR(N) = I(A/P, i, N) − SN(A/F, i, N)

Economic service life = the number of years N that gives the lowest EUAC. As years go by, the capital cost per year falls but the running cost rises, so the total has a minimum.

Rule: replace now if EUAC of the defender (over its best remaining life) > EUAC of the challenger (at its economic life). Otherwise keep the old one for now.

9Inflation
Actual vs constant rupees, market and inflation-free interest rates
Formulas
Actual (then-current) rupees

The money you will really pay or receive at that time, including inflation.

Constant (real) rupees

The same money shown in today’s buying power (inflation removed).

Actual = Constant × (1 + f)n
Market rate: im = i′ + f + i′·f i.e. (1+im) = (1+i′)(1+f)
